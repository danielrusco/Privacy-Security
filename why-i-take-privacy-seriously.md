# Why I Take Privacy Seriously (and Think You Should Too)

There's a question I keep coming back to. Not as an abstract puzzle, but as something I actually need an answer to: where, in a life lived mostly through screens, am I supposed to do the work of becoming a person?

The work I mean is the slow, unglamorous work of figuring out what you actually think — not what you'd say at a dinner party, not what you'd post, but what you'd admit to yourself in a quiet room with no one listening. The work of trying on an idea and discarding it. Of changing your mind. Of being uncertain about something for a long time before you settle. Philosophers and sociologists who've thought hard about this have a striking agreement: that work needs somewhere to happen. It needs a back room. A space where you're not being watched, not being measured, not being shaped by the awareness that someone else is taking notes. When you take that space away from people — and we know this from studying prisons and asylums, places designed to leave inmates nowhere unobserved — their selves don't grow. They don't even hold together. Privacy isn't about hiding. It's the condition under which there's a self to have at all.

The thing about modern online life is that it quietly takes the back room away. Not all at once, and not in any single dramatic act — just steadily. Every search you make, every article you linger on, every link you almost click gets folded into a profile that exists somewhere you can't see, and that profile gets used to shape what you encounter next. None of it feels like surveillance because none of it announces itself. It just shows up as a feed that keeps getting eerily good at holding your attention, an ad that knows what you were thinking about, a recommendation that seems to know you better than you know yourself. The slow effect of all this isn't that someone learns your secrets. It's that the line between *who you are* and *who the algorithm has nudged you to be* starts to blur. You stop being able to tell which of your wants are yours. The back room gets renovated by people you've never met, working from data you never knowingly gave them.

I think most of us feel this even when we don't have words for it. There's a low-grade unease in modern digital life that I don't think is paranoia. I think it's the felt sense of being measured all the time, by systems that don't care about you in any direction — they just want to predict what you'll do next so someone can sell against it. You can get used to that feeling, but getting used to it isn't the same as it being okay.

So when I take privacy seriously, I'm not protecting secrets. I'm protecting the conditions under which I can still be the author of my own life. That doesn't mean going off-grid or treating every app like an enemy. It means noticing that the back room matters — that some part of your inner life needs to stay yours, untracked, unindexed, unmeasured — and making small, deliberate choices to defend it. Use a browser that doesn't sell you out. Turn off the tracking you can turn off. Pay for the things that would otherwise pay themselves by harvesting you. Treat your attention and your data as if they belonged to you, because the alternative is letting someone else decide who you get to become.
